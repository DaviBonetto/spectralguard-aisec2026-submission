\begin{abstract}
Hidden-state poisoning can corrupt the recurrent memory of state-space
language models, yet a detector's aggregate score does not establish preserved
task behavior or operational availability. We introduce \emph{DURA}, an
evaluation methodology that separates Detection, Utility, Robustness, and
Availability. First, an exact forgetting certificate and a constructive
counterexample delimit what transition spectral radius can and cannot certify.
Then, a leakage-controlled campaign spanning seven checkpoints and four tasks
tests a frozen layer-wise spectral monitor against progressively stronger
attackers and a representation-based comparator. The spectral monitor reaches
F1~$0.806$ (95\% cluster-bootstrap CI $[0.795,0.818]$), but a common-protocol
BOE+XGBoost comparator reaches mean F1~$0.969$. Clean task accuracy is already
only $13/300$ ($4.33\%$); under a detector-aware attack it is $5/300$
($1.67\%$). Under a validation-selected graduated
policy, just 8 of 320 clean sessions survive. Thus spectral dynamics contain
detection signal, but separability does not imply robust or available defense.
DURA exposes this distinction and provides an auditable template for evaluating
hidden-state monitors beyond headline F1 and ROC-AUC.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
State-space language models (SSMs) replace quadratic attention with a recurrent
state that supports efficient long-sequence processing. That state is also a
security boundary. A short hostile insertion can alter future computation even
after the inserted text is no longer prominent in the visible context. Hidden
State Poisoning Attacks (HiSPAs) demonstrate this failure in Mamba-family
models and hybrid architectures \cite{lemercier2026hispa}. The natural defense
response is to monitor internal dynamics. The natural evaluation mistake is to
equate a monitor's test-set discrimination with a usable defense.

Consider a detector with high area under the ROC curve. It may still miss an
attacker that optimizes against its score, block benign work at an unacceptable
rate, or preserve a high detection rate while the underlying task has already
collapsed. These are distinct failures with distinct units of analysis. The
adversarial-ML literature has repeatedly shown that static or weakly tuned
evaluations can overstate robustness \cite{carlini2017towards,
carlini2017detectors,athalye2018obfuscated,croce2020autoattack}. Security
operations adds another constraint: even accurate alarms can be unusable when
base rates, alert volume, and response policies are ignored
\cite{axelsson2000baserate,alahmadi2022falsepositives}. Hidden-state poisoning
requires both lessons at once.

We study SpectralGuard, a frozen monitor built from layer-wise trajectories of
the discretized state-transition spectral radius. Rather than present it as a
new state-of-the-art defense, we use it to ask a harder question: \emph{what
evidence is necessary before an internal monitor deserves to be called a
defense?} Our answer is DURA---a four-axis methodology that separately measures
\textbf{D}etection, attacked-task \textbf{U}tility, adaptive
\textbf{R}obustness, and session-level \textbf{A}vailability. Figure
\ref{fig:overview} makes the evaluation boundary explicit.

We answer four research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} what does spectral radius
certify, and what failure is invisible to a radius-only monitor? \textbf{RQ2:}
how much held-out detection signal do layer-wise spectral features provide
relative to a representation-based comparator? \textbf{RQ3:} how does the
frozen monitor behave as attacker knowledge increases, and is task utility
preserved? \textbf{RQ4:} can a response policy sustain benign multi-turn
sessions at acceptable cost? The study uses disjoint calibration, validation,
and test roles; frozen prompt clusters; immutable model revisions; and explicit
uncertainty whose interpretation matches its source.

This paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A theory boundary.} We derive an exact forgetting certificate
  for frozen diagonal transitions and prove that, in dimension at least two,
  no transition-radius-only readout guarantee can be unconditional. A
  1,000-instance constructive test illustrates the blind spot.
  \item \textbf{The DURA methodology.} We operationalize four distinct defense
  claims across seven checkpoints, four tasks, common splits, transfer and
  adaptive attackers, matched benign controls, and multi-turn sessions.
  \item \textbf{An operational finding.} Spectral features are predictive, but
  the representation comparator is stronger, detector-aware attacks leave task
  accuracy near the floor, and both response policies largely deny service to
  benign sessions. The monitor is therefore evidence of signal, not evidence
  of a deployable defense.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{State-space sequence models and their security surface}
Transformers obtain flexible content-based interactions at quadratic attention
cost \cite{vaswani2017attention}. Structured SSMs instead express sequence
processing through linear dynamics and efficient convolution or recurrence.
S4 made long-range structured transitions practical
\cite{gu2022s4}; S5 simplified the construction to a multi-input,
multi-output layer \cite{smith2023s5}; and Hyena explored subquadratic long
convolutions with data-controlled gating \cite{poli2023hyena}. Mamba makes
SSM parameters input-dependent, selecting what to propagate or forget while
retaining linear scaling \cite{gu2024mamba}. Mamba-2 relates selective SSMs
to structured semiseparable matrices through state-space duality
\cite{dao2024mamba2}. These architectures expose internal recurrent dynamics
whose persistence is not captured by inspecting visible tokens alone.

HiSPA targets that internal surface: adversarial text corrupts recurrent memory
and affects later task behavior \cite{lemercier2026hispa}. CLASP detects such
attacks from block-output embeddings (BOEs) using token- and document-level
classifiers \cite{lemercier2026clasp}. Our work is complementary in mechanism
and different in objective. SpectralGuard observes transition dynamics rather
than BOEs; DURA asks whether either kind of signal survives stronger attackers,
preserves the task, and supports an operational response policy.

\subsection{Internal and anomaly detection}
Anomaly and out-of-distribution detection often uses confidence, feature, or
energy statistics \cite{hendrycks2017baseline,liang2018odin,liu2020energy}.
Raw scores are not automatically calibrated probabilities
\cite{guo2017calibration}, and selective prediction explicitly studies the
risk--coverage tradeoff induced by abstention \cite{geifman2017selective}.
Backdoor defenses likewise inspect model behavior or input anomalies: Neural
Cleanse reverse-engineers class-specific triggers \cite{wang2019neuralcleanse},
STRIP measures prediction entropy under input perturbations
\cite{gao2019strip}, and ONION filters anomalous words in textual inputs
\cite{qi2021onion}. These methods motivate internal monitoring, but their
existence also warns against treating one statistic as a complete security
invariant.

\subsection{Adaptive and operational evaluation}
Adversarial examples show that empirical accuracy under a fixed attack is not a
robustness certificate \cite{goodfellow2015explaining,carlini2017towards}.
Detector bypass and gradient obfuscation work sharpen the requirement: the
attacker must know the claimed defense and optimize against it
\cite{carlini2017detectors,athalye2018obfuscated}. AutoAttack further shows how
attack choice and tuning alter apparent robustness \cite{croce2020autoattack}.
DURA therefore escalates from transfer to query and joint white-box access
without refitting the detector.

Deployment units matter too. Closed-world benchmark assumptions can fail for
network intrusion detection \cite{sommer2010closedworld}; base rates can make
small false-positive rates operationally dominant
\cite{axelsson2000baserate}; and common ML-security pitfalls include leakage,
weak baselines, and lab-only evaluation \cite{arp2022dosdonts}. SOC analysts
report the direct cost of noisy alarms \cite{alahmadi2022falsepositives}, while
connection-level SSH detection demonstrates the value of evaluating interactive
units rather than isolated records \cite{piet2023ssh}. We make no general
priority claim for session-level evaluation. Our narrower contribution is to
place benign session survival alongside detection, task utility, and adaptive
evasion for hidden-state poisoning.

\section{Threat Model and DURA Questions}
The protected system is an autoregressive Mamba-family language model whose
recurrent state persists across an intervening hostile message and a later task
query. The defender can observe internal activations and discretized diagonal
transition parameters, but does not modify model weights. The monitor and its
threshold are frozen before final testing. A response policy may block a
session after alerts; it cannot recover a corrupted state.

\paragraph{Attacker ladder.}
We consider four access levels. A \emph{transfer} attacker crafts against a
surrogate or fixed task loss. A \emph{query} attacker receives monitor outcomes
and uses at most 256 detector queries; the realized candidate count has median
80 and 95th percentile 137.6. A \emph{joint white-box} attacker differentiates
through task and detector objectives, using three restarts and spectral-loss
weights $\lambda\in\{0.25,1,2\}$. An \emph{adversarial-benign} attacker searches
for innocuous-looking text that triggers blocking while preserving the benign
task intent. We also include original HiSPA, gradient-guided, matched benign,
and clean controls. These levels test different claims; none is relabeled as a
formal worst-case guarantee.

\paragraph{Defense claims and acceptance units.}
RQ1 is analytic and constructive: the unit is a dynamical system. RQ2 uses
prompt-cluster-disjoint held-out rows. RQ3 uses paired attacked prompts and a
frozen detector. RQ4 uses three-turn sessions and windows. A model can pass one
question and fail another. Precision--recall and ROC summaries are both
reported because their interpretations differ under class imbalance
\cite{davis2006prroc}.

\section{Spectral Foundations: RQ1}
For one frozen token transition, let
\begin{equation}
 \bar A=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_d),\qquad
 0<\rho=\max_i|a_i|<1.
\end{equation}
For an unforced state $h_t=\bar A^t h_0$ and $0<\epsilon<1$, define
\begin{equation}
 H_\epsilon(h_0)=\min\{t\ge0:\|\bar A^t h_0\|_2
 \le\epsilon\|h_0\|_2\}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Exact diagonal forgetting certificate]
\label{thm:forgetting}
For $h_t=\bar A^t h_0$,
\begin{equation}
 \|h_t\|_2^2=\sum_i |a_i|^{2t}|h_{0,i}|^2
 \le\rho^{2t}\|h_0\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
 H_\epsilon(h_0)\le
 \left\lceil\frac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{\log(1/\rho)}\right\rceil.
\label{eq:certificate}
\end{equation}
If $P_\rho$ projects onto coordinates with $|a_i|=\rho$
and $\alpha=\|P_\rho h_0\|_2/\|h_0\|_2>0$, then $\|h_t\|_2\ge\alpha\rho^t\|h_0\|_2$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is coordinate-wise and appears in Appendix
\ref{app:diagonal-proof}. Equation \eqref{eq:certificate} is an upper bound on
forgetting time; it is not a lower bound on remembered task information. If
$\rho=1-\eta$ and $\eta\rightarrow0^+$, the bound scales as $O(1/\eta)$, but
the exact logarithmic denominator is used outside that near-critical regime.
For the selective time-varying system, the unforced contribution is
$\prod_{s=1}^t\bar A_s h_0$ and is bounded by
$\prod_s\rho(\bar A_s)\|h_0\|_2$; a single frozen $\rho$ is only a local
approximation.

\begin{proposition}[No unconditional radius-only readout certificate]
\label{prop:impossibility}
For $d\ge2$, fix any stable diagonal $\bar A$ and nonzero retained state $h$.
There exist unit-norm readouts $C$ and $C'$ such that $Ch\ne0$, $C'h=0$, and
the entire transition spectrum---hence every statistic computed only from
$\bar A$---is identical. Therefore no monitor whose input is only the
transition spectral radius can unconditionally certify preservation of an
arbitrary linear readout.
\end{proposition}

Choose $C=h^\top/\|h\|_2$ and choose a unit vector $C'$ in the nonempty
orthogonal complement of $h$. Both systems have the same $\bar A$ and radius,
but opposite readout outcomes. This analytic two-dimensional construction is
not a claim that arbitrary readout rotations are reachable from discrete
tokens; it separates \emph{information monitored} from \emph{information
needed for a universal guarantee}.

The constructive test holds $\bar A$ bitwise fixed, rotates a unit-norm readout
orthogonal to the retained state, and repeats this operation over 1,000 seeded
diagonal systems. The output falls by 100\% in every witness, while the monitor
detects 0/1,000 (95\% Wilson upper bound 0.38\%). A matched transition-collapse
control is detected in 1,000/1,000 (lower bound 99.62\%).

\answerbox{RQ1}{For frozen diagonal transitions, radius exactly bounds decay of
the unforced state. It cannot unconditionally certify arbitrary readout or task
preservation; an analytic $d\ge2$ counterexample and 1,000 constructive systems
expose that blind spot.}

\section{Monitor and Experimental Methodology}
\subsection{Frozen monitor and response policies}
For each observed token and each of 64 monitored Mamba layers, SpectralGuard
extracts the maximum magnitude of the diagonal discretized transition. Each
layer trajectory is reduced to one temporal mean, yielding 64 ordered
layer-mean radius features. A deterministic logistic
regression is fit on calibration data; standardization, coefficients, and
schema are then frozen. Validation data selects the decision threshold. Test
labels do not select features, coefficients, thresholds, attack parameters, or
policies.

More precisely, for token $t$ and monitored layer $\ell$, let
$\bar A_{t,\ell}$ denote the input-conditioned discretized transition and
define the observed radius
\begin{equation}
  r_{t,\ell}=\rho(\bar A_{t,\ell})
  =\max_j |(\bar A_{t,\ell})_{jj}|.
  \label{eq:observed-radius}
\end{equation}
For each of the 64 monitored layers, the trajectory $\{r_{t,\ell}\}_t$ is
reduced to its temporal mean $x_\ell=T^{-1}\sum_t r_{t,\ell}$. Thus
$x=(x_0,\ldots,x_{63})\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$, serialized in the exact order
\texttt{layer\_0\_rho\_mean} through \texttt{layer\_63\_rho\_mean}.
Calibration fixes a
standardizer $(\mu,\sigma)$ and a logistic score
\begin{equation}
  q(x)=\operatorname{sigmoid}\!\left(
  b+\theta^\top\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
  \label{eq:frozen-monitor}
\end{equation}
The frozen threshold is $\tau=0.458$; an observation alerts when
$q(x)\ge\tau$. Missing or non-finite telemetry fails closed rather than being
silently imputed. Equations~\eqref{eq:observed-radius}
and~\eqref{eq:frozen-monitor} define the complete learned monitor used in every
test cell; no online update, architecture-specific refit, or post-hoc threshold
change is permitted.

Two response policies consume the same frozen alerts. \emph{Fail-closed}
blocks a session after the first alert. \emph{Graduated blocking} triggers only
after at least $k$ alerts within a window of $w$ observations. The pair
$(k,w)=(4,4)$ is selected on 60 validation sessions from the grid
$k\in\{2,3,4\}$, $w\in\{4,8,16\}$ subject to fixed attack-capture constraints.
It is not tuned on the 320-session test set. Operational evaluation uses
three-turn sessions, 32-token monitoring windows, and a stride of eight tokens.
We report both
session survival and blocked-window rate: the former asks whether a user can
finish, whereas the latter measures how much work the policy suppresses.

\subsection{Models, tasks, and frozen roles}
The evaluation uses associative recall, GSM8K, HumanEval, and LongBench Qasper.
They provide exact-match memory, mathematical reasoning, code generation, and
long-context question answering. Low clean performance on some task--model
pairs is reported rather than filtered away; this becomes important when
interpreting attack success.

\subsection{Splits, comparators, and accounting}
Prompt identifiers, task labels, and split assignments are frozen before any
fit. The common detector comparison uses 1,200 calibration, 800 validation,
and 4,000 held-out rows: 1,000 prompt clusters, each evaluated under clean,
HiSPA-original, gradient-guided, and adaptive-v2 conditions. No prompt cluster
crosses split roles. The broader confirmatory campaign contains 19,300 raw rows
across fixed attacks, task controls, transfer models, and adaptive cells. The
five prescribed labels are 42, 123, 456, 789, and 2026.

The primary comparator is a CLASP-style BOE+XGBoost implementation using block
embeddings and block statistics under the same calibration, validation, and
test roles \cite{lemercier2026clasp}. It is a design-faithful reimplementation,
not the authors' official pretrained release. We also isolate mean radius,
layer-wise spectral features, and matched lexical controls. This hierarchy
tests whether any advantage comes from a scalar, a richer transition
representation, or a general hidden representation.

\subsection{Metrics and uncertainty}
Detection uses precision, recall, F1, and ROC-AUC. SpectralGuard is deterministic
on the fixed data, so repeating its fit under five seed labels does not create
five independent samples. Its uncertainty is a 2,000-resample prompt-cluster
bootstrap over the fixed held-out set. XGBoost varies with training seed on
the same split; we report the mean and a descriptive $t$ interval across the
five fits. These intervals answer different questions and are never plotted as
exchangeable error bars.

Task utility uses task-specific score and paired correctness. The v2--v3
comparison reports a 2,000-resample paired interval and exact McNemar test.
Multiple exploratory comparisons use Benjamini--Hochberg control
\cite{benjamini1995fdr}. Session results use empirical survival, hard-block and
blocked-window rates with session bootstrap intervals. Runtime includes model
generation and monitoring, not only classifier inference.

\section{DURA Evaluation}
\subsection{RQ2: How much detection signal is present?}
Table \ref{tab:detectors} reports the common-protocol result. SpectralGuard
separates the four conditions substantially better than chance: precision
0.775, recall 0.840, F1~0.806, and ROC-AUC~0.887. Its fixed-test prompt-cluster bootstrap intervals are narrow enough
to rule out sampling noise as the explanation for the signal. However, the
BOE+XGBoost comparator is materially stronger: precision 0.960, recall 0.978,
mean F1~0.969, and ROC-AUC~0.997 over
five training-seed fits on the same shared split. This is not a tie and not a
SpectralGuard superiority claim.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What the DURA matrix changes}
If evaluation stopped at RQ2, SpectralGuard would look promising: F1~0.806 and
ROC-AUC~0.887 are real held-out signal. The remaining axes reverse the deployment
conclusion. A stronger representation reaches F1~0.969, adaptive access lowers
the spectral detector to F1~0.746, task accuracy remains near zero, and the
response policy blocks nearly every benign session. DURA does not average
these outcomes into a composite score because such an average would permit a
strong detector to compensate numerically for denial of service. Instead, it
exposes a vector of claims whose deployment acceptability must be specified in
advance.

The result is not that spectral monitoring is useless. Transition telemetry is
cheap to define, mechanistically interpretable in the frozen diagonal case,
and predictively useful. It may complement richer representations, support
diagnosis, or act as one feature in a calibrated ensemble. The result is that
it cannot carry the full semantic burden assigned to ``defense.'' The theory
shows why: contraction is only one property of the state-to-output computation.
The experiments show how the missing dimensions become concrete under
representation comparison, adaptive optimization, and response policy.

\subsection{Design implications}
First, future monitors should combine transition, reachability, and readout
information rather than search for a universal scalar. BOE performance suggests
that richer internal representations contain discriminative signal unavailable
to radius alone. Second, task-preserving recovery deserves separate design
attention. Blocking after detection may prevent continuation, but it does not
restore a corrupted recurrent state. State reset, checkpointed rollback, or
selective recomputation could turn detection into recovery, provided their own
utility and cost are measured. Third, policies need explicit service-level
constraints. A validation objective that preserves attack capture but allows
6.67\% clean survival on validation can still collapse to 2.5\% on test; this
is a warning that the feasible region itself may be unacceptable.

\subsection{How to interpret the comparator}
The CLASP-style result is a common-protocol comparator, not a leaderboard claim
against the official CLASP release. We reproduce the published design elements
with BOEs, block statistics, and XGBoost, while holding our split and test
conditions fixed. This isolates representation quality within our campaign.
It does not establish that our implementation, corpus, attacks, or hyperparameters
are identical to the authors'. Conversely, its strong result is still a valid
negative finding for SpectralGuard: under the protocol we control, a richer
representation separates the same held-out rows much better.

\subsection{Acceptance criteria and negative results}
The four DURA axes are deliberately not interchangeable. A monitor may pass a
discrimination criterion while failing task preservation or availability; a
low-latency policy may still be unusable if its benign blocking rate is high.
Accordingly, we do not declare a global pass from an average, rank, or composite
score. A deployment claim would require, at minimum, a threshold selected
without test labels, resilience to the strongest declared attacker, useful
clean and attacked task performance, and a response point compatible with an
explicit service-level constraint. SpectralGuard does not meet that joint bar
in this campaign.

This interpretation also prevents three common denominator errors. First,
conditional ASR is meaningful only where the clean answer is correct; it cannot
be generalized from 13 eligible prompts to all 300 adaptive prompts. Second,
perfect malicious-session blocking is not a security success when nearly all
benign sessions are denied as well. Third, a narrow bootstrap interval around
F1 quantifies uncertainty on the fixed prompt population, not robustness to a
new attack, model, or workload. These are limitations on the scope of the
evidence, not reasons to discard the measured signal. The negative deployment
result is therefore a reproducible outcome of the evaluation criteria rather
than an absence of experimental evidence.

\section{Limitations}
\paragraph{Task floors.}
Clean accuracy is low for several task--model pairs, especially HumanEval and
GSM8K. We retain those rows to expose the denominator rather than select only
successful prompts. The consequence is equally important: the campaign cannot
estimate robust task utility in regions where the base model rarely succeeds.
Future work needs stronger task-matched checkpoints and larger clean-correct
subsets before making conditional attack-success claims.

\paragraph{Synthetic sessions.}
The three-turn sessions provide a controlled unit for response-policy stress
testing, not a production traffic model. They cannot estimate a production
false-block rate, analyst workload, or user abandonment. Real traffic, longer
histories, prevalence shifts, and operator studies are necessary before any
deployment claim. The present result is narrower: the tested policies fail even
under this controlled benign workload.

\paragraph{Architecture and access.}
The theory applies exactly to frozen diagonal transitions and bounds only the
unforced state contribution. Selective time variation, driven inputs, hybrid
attention paths, quantization, and alternative SSM parameterizations require
separate analysis. The adaptive attacks are strong within declared budgets but
do not constitute exhaustive search or certified worst cases.

\paragraph{Uncertainty and generalization.}
The SpectralGuard bootstrap describes sampling uncertainty on one fixed test
set; the comparator interval describes seed-to-seed training variation on that
same split, not independent sampling uncertainty. Neither establishes
population-level generalization across domains.
The five seed labels attached to a deterministic fit are not independent
replicates. Cross-model controls broaden failure analysis but do not transform
the common-protocol Mamba result into a universal statement.

\section{Conclusion}
We evaluated spectral monitoring for hidden-state poisoning as a defense claim,
not only a classification task. The resulting DURA methodology separates four
questions that aggregate detector scores conflate. Spectral radius provides an
exact frozen-diagonal forgetting certificate, yet a simple readout construction
proves that it cannot unconditionally certify task preservation. In held-out
experiments, layer-wise spectral features provide genuine detection signal but
trail a representation-based comparator. Stronger attacker access erodes the
frozen detector, attacked-task utility remains at the floor, and both tested
response policies nearly eliminate benign availability. The central lesson is
therefore constructive: internal monitoring should be judged by what it
detects, what behavior survives, how it responds to adaptation, and whether a
user can still complete a session. Detection is evidence; defense is the joint
claim.

\section{Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:forgetting}}
\label{app:diagonal-proof}
For diagonal $\bar A$,
\[
 \bar A^t h_0=(a_1^t h_{0,1},\ldots,a_d^t h_{0,d})^\top.
\]
Taking the squared Euclidean norm yields
\[
 \|\bar A^t h_0\|_2^2=\sum_i|a_i|^{2t}|h_{0,i}|^2
 \le\rho^{2t}\sum_i|h_{0,i}|^2.
\]
Thus $\|\bar A^t h_0\|_2\le\rho^t\|h_0\|_2$. Requiring
$\rho^t\le\epsilon$ and using $0<\rho<1$ gives
$t\ge\log(1/\epsilon)/\log(1/\rho)$ and the integer ceiling in
Equation \eqref{eq:certificate}. If $P_\rho$ selects coordinates attaining
$\rho$, then their contribution alone has norm
$\rho^t\|P_\rho h_0\|_2=\alpha\rho^t\|h_0\|_2$, proving the lower bound.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
The experiments use public benchmarks and public model checkpoints; no human
subjects or private user conversations are included. The work studies attacks
that could induce denial of service. Its defensive benefit is diagnostic: exposing when
detection coexists with task failure or benign blocking. Its dual-use risk is
monitor evasion or deliberate false alarms. We mitigate that risk with bounded
public checkpoints and declared budgets, and release no credential, private
trace, live endpoint, or automation against a deployed service. This follows
the Menlo principles of beneficence and respect for law and public interest
\cite{bailey2012menlo}.

No human participant, private data, live target, live vulnerability, or undisclosed
vendor flaw was studied; no IRB/ERB or vendor-specific disclosure process was
applicable. Findings concern controlled sessions, not a product.
Future product validation should require authorization, minimize user impact,
and coordinate disclosure before publishing exploit details.
